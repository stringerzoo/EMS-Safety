EMS Helicopter Safety

Is flying in an EMS helicopter really safer than driving?

The short answer is yes — and the data back it up. This page explains the research, the numbers, and the reasoning behind that claim, with links to every primary source.

Overview

Key findings

When you compare fatal accident rates per mile traveled — the most meaningful unit for evaluating transportation risk — an EMS helicopter is one of the safest ways to move between two points. The data come from two independent federal sources and point to the same conclusion:

  1. An EMS helicopter is approximately 2× safer per mile than riding in a passenger car.
  2. An EMS helicopter is approximately 63× safer per mile than riding a motorcycle.

The sections below explain where each number comes from, what assumptions were required to produce a fair comparison, and where the data have honest limitations. A printable summary of the key statistics is also available as a half-sheet infographic.

Why "per mile" and not "per trip" or "per hour"?
Per-mile is the most meaningful unit for comparing transportation modes because it normalizes for the fact that different vehicles travel at very different speeds. A helicopter covers roughly 130 miles in the same hour a car covers 35–65 miles on a typical commute. Per-mile comparison asks: for each mile of travel, how likely is a fatal outcome? This is the same unit used by NHTSA for all road vehicle comparisons, making it the most apples-to-apples metric available.

Section 1

Helicopter safety data

Primary source: U.S. Helicopter Safety Team (USHST)

The U.S. Helicopter Safety Team is a joint government-industry body comprising the FAA, helicopter manufacturers, operators, and safety organizations. It publishes annual fatal accident rates for all U.S. civil helicopter operations, derived from NTSB accident reports and FAA flight hour surveys.

The rate used in the infographic is the 5-year average from 2020 through 2024: 0.65 fatal accidents per 100,000 flight hours. This is the most statistically stable figure available — single-year rates can fluctuate significantly given the small absolute number of accidents.

Why use the 5-year average rather than the most recent year?
2024's rate of 0.44 is encouraging, but it reflects only 13 fatal accidents across the entire U.S. fleet — a small enough number that year-to-year statistical noise is significant. The 5-year average of 0.65 is a more conservative and defensible figure. It is also the basis on which USHST sets its published safety goals.

What these rates cover

USHST rates cover all U.S. civil helicopter operations — agricultural, utility, tour, personal, and air medical. EMS-specific (Part 135 air medical) rates have historically tracked close to the fleet average. In 2024, the Part 135 rate was 0.46 per 100,000 hours, slightly above the Part 91 (private) rate of 0.34 — making the fleet-average figure a reasonable and slightly conservative proxy for EMS operations specifically.

Converting flight hours to miles

USHST publishes rates per flight hour. To compare to road vehicles (reported per mile), a cruise speed assumption is required. The Bell 407 — a representative single-engine turbine EMS helicopter — cruises at approximately 130 mph (113 knots) in still air. This is a well-established published performance figure.

0.65 fatal accidents / 100,000 flight hours × 130 miles / hour = 0.65 fatal accidents / 13,000,000 miles Scaled to per-100-million-miles: 0.65 × (100,000,000 / 13,000,000) = ~0.50 fatal accidents per 100M miles

Section 2

Road vehicle safety data

Primary source: NHTSA Fatality Analysis Reporting System (FARS)

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's FARS is a census — not a sample — of all fatal traffic crashes on U.S. public roads. It has been collected since 1975 and is the authoritative source for U.S. road fatality statistics. Vehicle miles traveled (VMT) figures come from the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA).

All road figures below are from the NHTSA Traffic Safety Facts 2023 Data: Motorcycles publication.

Motorcycle data: additional context

The motorcycle figure of 31.39 per 100M miles represents the average across all motorcycle types and riders. The risk is not uniform:

  • Supersport motorcycles (high-performance, track-derived) have a fatal rate roughly 4× higher than cruisers or standard bikes, per IIHS research.
  • Speeding was a factor in 36% of fatal motorcycle crashes in 2023, compared to 22% for passenger car drivers.
  • Alcohol impairment was present in 26% of fatally injured motorcycle riders; 40% in single-vehicle crashes.
  • Helmet use reduces fatality risk by approximately 37% (NHTSA estimate). In 2023, 35% of riders killed were not wearing helmets.

These factors mean a responsible, experienced, helmeted rider on a standard motorcycle has meaningfully lower individual risk than the fleet average — but the fleet average is the appropriate figure for a general comparison.

Section 3

The comparison: per hour vs. per mile

The two metrics tell meaningfully different stories, and both are valid depending on what question you're asking.

Per hour of exposure

This asks: while I am actively engaged in this activity, how dangerous is each hour? On this basis, the helicopter (0.65 per 100K hours) is roughly 2–3× safer than a motorcycle (~1.19 per 100K hours, estimated). The motorcycle figure requires converting NHTSA's VMT data to hours using an assumed average riding speed of ~38 mph.

Per mile traveled

This asks: for each mile I need to cover, how dangerous is each transportation choice? Because a helicopter travels at ~130 mph versus a motorcycle's ~38 mph average, the helicopter covers roughly 3.4× more distance per hour. This multiplies its safety advantage: the helicopter goes from 2–3× safer per hour to approximately 63× safer per mile versus a motorcycle, and approximately 2× safer per mile versus a passenger car.

The per-mile metric is the most meaningful for a patient considering "how risky is this transport?" because the relevant question is covering the distance to the hospital safely — not how many hours are spent in transit.
Section 4

Assumptions and extrapolations

Two calculations in this comparison require assumptions not directly stated in the source data. Both are clearly flagged here.

Assumption 1
Helicopter cruise speed: ~130 mph

Required to convert the USHST's flight-hour rate to a per-mile rate. The Bell 407 — a common EMS platform — has a published cruise speed of approximately 113–120 knots (130–138 mph). We used 130 mph as a conservative round figure. This assumption has low sensitivity: even at 100 mph cruise, the helicopter's per-mile rate (~0.65) remains well below the passenger car rate (1.13).

Assumption 2
Motorcycle average speed: ~38 mph

Required to convert NHTSA's VMT-based motorcycle fatality rate to a per-hour rate for the time-exposure comparison. NHTSA does not publish motorcycle fatality rates per hour. The 38 mph figure reflects a blended average across urban stop-and-go, suburban, and highway riding — consistent with FHWA traffic speed data for motorcycles. This assumption only affects the per-hour comparison; the per-mile comparison uses NHTSA's published VMT figure directly and requires no speed assumption for motorcycles.

Section 5

Important caveats

Risk is not uniformly distributed across a flight

The per-mile metric implicitly assumes risk is constant across all miles flown. In practice, helicopter risk is concentrated in specific phases of operation — particularly low-altitude maneuvering, approach to off-airport landing zones, and flight in poor visibility. Cruise at altitude (the majority of miles covered) is substantially safer than the terminal regime. This means the per-mile figure somewhat overstates how dangerous the "cruise" portion of a flight is, while understating risk during takeoff and landing.

Fleet-average rates vs. individual operator rates

USHST rates cover all U.S. civil helicopter operators. Safety performance varies by operator, with well-run Part 135 air medical programs — which have mandatory safety management systems, flight risk assessment tools, and weather minimums — performing at or better than the fleet average. Conversely, some segments of the civil fleet (agricultural operations, personal use) have higher rates.

The motorcycle rate is a population average

As noted above, the 31.39 figure covers all U.S. motorcyclists. An experienced, licensed, helmeted rider on a cruiser-style motorcycle in good conditions has meaningfully lower personal risk than this figure implies. The fleet average is appropriate for a general population comparison, not for assessing any individual rider's risk.

Both datasets improve over time

Helicopter safety has improved substantially over two decades; the 2024 rate of 0.44 may represent a new baseline rather than a statistical outlier. Using the 5-year average (0.65) is deliberately conservative. Similarly, motorcycle fatality rates have trended upward over the past decade, suggesting the 2023 figure may understate current risk.

Section 6

Why EMS helicopters are as safe as the data suggest

The safety record of air medical helicopters reflects structural features of the operating environment that go beyond pilot skill alone.

Three-crew operations

A typical EMS helicopter carries a pilot, a flight nurse, and a flight paramedic. All three are trained to monitor flight conditions and are empowered — and expected — to raise safety concerns at any point. This distributed responsibility structure is distinct from solo driving and is one reason aviation safety culture has historically produced lower accident rates than road transport.

Systematic safety management

Part 135 air medical operators are federally required to operate formal Safety Management Systems (SMS) — structured programs for identifying, tracking, and addressing hazards before they result in accidents. Every incident, equipment concern, and near-miss is logged and reviewed. This proactive approach is qualitatively different from road transport, where safety data are collected only after crashes occur.

Formal go/no-go decision-making

Every EMS flight begins with a structured Flight Risk Assessment Tool (FRAT) evaluation — a scored checklist that quantifies weather, crew fatigue, mission complexity, and other factors. Flights that exceed risk thresholds are declined or delayed. Drivers have no equivalent formal process before a commute.

Mandatory weather minimums

Part 135 operations require pilots to maintain defined minimum visibility and ceiling thresholds. Flight into weather below minimums is prohibited — not merely inadvisable. NTSB data show that unintended flight into poor visibility (inadvertent instrument meteorological conditions) is one of the leading causes of fatal helicopter accidents, and weather minimums are specifically designed to prevent it.

A sustained improvement trend

The U.S. helicopter fatal accident rate has declined from approximately 1.27 per 100,000 flight hours in the early 2000s to 0.44 in 2024 — a reduction of more than 65% over two decades. This reflects ongoing investment in training, technology (terrain awareness systems, autopilots, stability augmentation), and safety culture. The trend is not accidental; it is the result of systematic, industry-wide effort.
